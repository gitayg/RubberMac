# RubberMAC

Formerly Screen Tools. The log (`~/Library/Logs/ScreenTools.log`) and the data folders keep the old name.

A macOS menu-bar app (Swift/AppKit, no Xcode project) with Windows habits and display helpers. Clicking its menu-bar icon opens a settings panel with all of it:

- **Big Clock and pre-lock warning:** shows the Big Clock screen saver, warns (and dims the screens) before the screen saver locks.
- **Brightness:** sliders for each screen (DDC, or software dimming behind a DisplayLink dock), plus ⌃⌥⌘↑/↓.
- **Volume and brightness on the screen you're changing:** the volume keys change the volume themselves, in 16 steps (quarter steps with ⌥⇧), and show the level low on the screen under the pointer rather than under the menu-bar icon. With the pointer on an external screen whose brightness macOS can't set itself, the brightness keys change that screen's brightness (over DDC, or software dimming) and show it there; ⌃⌥⌘↑/↓ show it on each screen they change. The built-in screen, Apple's own displays and others macOS controls itself (like LG's UltraFine) keep macOS's brightness keys, a sound output whose volume can't be set (a TV on HDMI) keeps macOS's volume keys, and so do the keys pressed with ⌘, ⌃ or ⌥. A switch at the end of the Brightness page turns it off.
- **Real screen resolution:** the Brightness page lists, for each screen, the resolutions the Mac can really send to it, not only how big things look: the pixels sent, the refresh rate where a screen has more than one, what a HiDPI mode looks like, and which is the screen's native one, with the one in use ticked. Choosing one switches straight away and asks "Keep this resolution?" on every screen; Keep makes it stick, and Go Back (or ↩, Esc, or 15 seconds without an answer, in case the screen shows nothing) puts the old one back exactly.
- **Window snapping:** ⌘ + arrows snap to a grid you choose (2–4 columns × 1–3 rows), maximize and minimize, and move to the next screen; ⌘↑ pressed again on the window it just moved maximizes it, over the whole screen even where a camera stands in front of it. ⌘M minimizes all windows and ⌘⇧M brings them back.
- **Background windows:** dims or blurs everything behind the window you're using, on every screen and the desktop too, so it stands out; a Strength slider sets how much. The menu bar, the Dock, the window's sheets, its app's floating panels and RubberMAC's own panels stay clear. Clicks go through to the window underneath, and the effect follows as you switch apps or windows, minimize or close them. A full-screen app, or the desktop with nothing in front, is left alone. Off until you pick Dim or Blur on the Windows page. Blur uses a private macOS call, and dims instead if that call is ever missing.
- **Lost windows:** in the Lost Windows section of the Windows page, **Bring Windows Back Onto the Screens** moves every window on the desktop you're looking at that's mostly off the screens (under a third showing, or its title bar out of reach) or bigger than its screen back onto the screen holding most of it, shrinking it only as far as it must; windows that are fine aren't touched, and the row says how many moved. Windows on other desktops are left alone: switch to one and press again. **Forget an App's Window Sizes…** lists the apps that remember where their windows were; for the one you pick, after asking, it quits it (macOS asks once whether RubberMAC may), deletes the window frames it saved with `defaults`, as the Terminal fixes do, moves the windows macOS kept for it to the Trash and opens it again with fresh windows. Nothing else of the app changes, and an app that won't quit, asking about unsaved work say, is left as it was. Apps that keep their settings in a sandbox, like Photos and Preview, are listed only with Full Disk Access.
- **New windows on the screen you're using:** with two or more screens, **Open Apps and Files on the Screen You're Using**, in the New Windows section of the Windows page, moves a window that opens on another screen right after you open an app or a file (a Finder double-click, the Dock, Start or Spotlight) to the screen with the pointer, keeping its size and place, shrunk to fit if it must. Only the first windows in the next few seconds move; panels, dialogs, sheets, full-screen windows, a window you're dragging and a new tab in a window that was already open stay where they are. **Always Open an App on One Screen** gives an app a screen of its own: all its new windows open there, whenever they appear. A chosen screen that's unplugged shows as "(not connected)", and the app's windows open as usual until it's back. Off until you turn it on.
- **Dock in full-screen apps:** moving the pointer to the Dock's edge brings it up, without the firm push macOS wants there.
- **Pick a window from the Dock:** clicking the Dock icon of an app with two or more windows opens a menu of them beside the icon, each with a picture of the window and its title, instead of bringing the whole app forward; clicking one goes to that window, even if it's minimized, hidden or on another desktop. Esc, a click elsewhere or another click on the icon closes it. Apps with one window, ⌘/⌥/⌃/⇧-clicks, right-clicks and dragging icons work as before.
- **Keep the Dock on one screen:** with two or more screens, macOS moves the Dock to whichever screen's Dock edge the pointer pushes against. **Keep the Dock On**, on the Windows page, picks one screen for it. On the other screens the pointer stops four points short of the Dock's edge, so it can't push the Dock over. If the Dock is somewhere else when you choose, or after screens connect, RubberMAC brings it over by pushing on your screen's edge, moving the pointer there for a moment and back. It follows the Dock's side and auto-hide. A chosen screen that's unplugged shows as "(not connected)", and the Dock is left to macOS until it's back. With "Displays have separate Spaces" off the Dock can't leave the main screen, and the page says so.
- **Eye Color Camera:** recolours your eyes live on video calls, to a real eye colour (blue, ice blue, deep blue, blue green, green, gray, hazel, amber, brown) rather than a painted-on one. RubberMAC reads your camera, finds your irises with Apple's Vision framework, recolours them and sends the picture to OBS's virtual camera, all on this Mac; pick OBS Virtual Camera in Zoom, Meet or Teams. Its page in the settings panel turns it on and off, picks the colour from a row of colour circles in one click, sets the strength and camera, and can turn it on when RubberMAC starts. While it's on, the page also shows a preview of what others see, read from OBS Virtual Camera (never the real camera) and only while the page is open; it needs the camera permission. Needs [OBS](https://obsproject.com/download) installed, with Start Virtual Camera clicked in it once so macOS sets up its camera extension; OBS needn't be running after that. Until 1.26 this ran [camrelay](https://github.com/gitayg/camrelay), a Python helper, which is no longer needed; its colour, strength and camera carry over.
- **Camera in front of the screen:** mark where a camera on a stand covers part of a screen, and snapping keeps windows clear of it, except a second ⌘↑, which maximizes over it. A toggle turns this on and off.
- **PC keyboard habits:**
  - Alt-Tab switches windows (most recently used first, so a quick Alt-Tab goes back to the previous one; the order survives RubberMAC restarting) or apps. It lists the windows on every desktop and full-screen Space, and of hidden apps; picking one on another Space switches there. It shows a grid of window pictures sized so every window fits on the screen, each with its app icon, title and a small map of where the window is; clicking a tile switches to that window. Pictures need Screen Recording; without it each tile shows the window's place on the screens, with its app icon. In window mode ⌘Tab switches windows too, so each of an app's windows (say, three Teams windows) can be picked; a switch on the Keyboard page turns that off and gives ⌘Tab back to macOS.
  - Ctrl-C/X/V/Z copy, cut, paste and undo; Ctrl stays Ctrl in terminals, VS Code and remote desktops.
  - Ctrl-←/→ move by word, and Home/End go to the start and end of the line.
  - ⌃⌘←/→ goes to the desktop on the left or right, like Win+Ctrl+←/→, full-screen apps included: one per press, at once rather than sliding, and nothing at either end. Remote desktops and VMs keep the keys; a switch on the Keyboard page turns it off.
  - Left Alt-Shift switches the input language.
  - Each app keeps its own input language: switch to Hebrew in one app and English in another, however you switch (Alt-Shift, the menu bar's language menu, ⌃Space), and each is back in its own language when you return to it. An app you haven't switched in yet keeps whichever language is on. Spotlight opened with its shortcut, ⌘R or a ⌘ tap, and RubberMAC's Start, open in English (the ABC, U.S. or British layout you used last); close the search without opening an app and the app gets its language back, while an app opened from the search gets its own. Kept in `~/Library/Application Support/ScreenTools/input-per-app.json`; a switch on the Keyboard page turns each part off.
  - ⌘L locks the screen at once, like Win+L (the same call as Lock Screen in the Apple menu). It takes ⌘L from every app, so a browser's ⌘L for the address bar stops working; a switch on the Keyboard page turns it off.
  - ⌘R opens Spotlight's search bar, like Win+R, by pressing Spotlight's own shortcut (⌘Space unless you changed it; with that shortcut turned off, ⌘R stays the app's). It takes ⌘R from every app but remote desktops and VMs, so a browser's reload and Mail's reply on ⌘R stop working; a switch on the Keyboard page turns it off.
  - ⌘ tapped on its own opens RubberMAC's Start (below), or Spotlight's Apps view with Start turned off, like the Windows key opens Start: either ⌘, pressed and let go with no other key, modifier, click or scroll in between. Remote desktops and VMs keep it; it does nothing while Secure Input is on, since the keys pressed with ⌘ are hidden then; a switch on the Keyboard page turns it off.
  - RubberMAC's Start comes up in the middle of the screen with the pointer, above everything, while the app you were in stays in front. It lists every app in Applications, System Applications and your own Applications folder, Utilities included. Type to search at once: each word you type matches the start of a word in an app's name, so "code" finds Visual Studio Code, whatever the case or accents; ↩ opens the first match, or the one the arrow keys move to. Drag icons into your own order, drop one app onto another to make a folder (called Folder until you click its name and type another), and drag apps back out; a folder left with one app becomes that app again. New apps go at the end and removed ones disappear. Right-click an app to open it, pin it to a row at the top (in an order you drag), show it in Finder, or move it to the Trash, after a question that names it; apps that came with macOS can't be removed and don't offer it. Another ⌘ tap, Esc or a click elsewhere closes it. Your order, folders and pins are kept in `~/Library/Application Support/ScreenTools/start.json`; **⌘ Alone Opens RubberMAC's Start, Not Spotlight's Apps**, on the Keyboard page, goes back to Spotlight's Apps view.
  - **Spotlight Searches Only Apps**, on the Keyboard page, makes Spotlight's search bar show apps and nothing else, as ⌘1 in it does (the tip from the MacRumors thread): each time the bar opens, with ⌘Space, ⌘R or otherwise, RubberMAC sends ⌘1 to Spotlight once the bar's search field has the keyboard, and Spotlight shows its Back button and an empty field reading "Applications". The ⌘1 goes to Spotlight alone, never to another app. Spotlight wipes a letter that reaches it while it switches to apps, so RubberMAC holds the keys typed into Spotlight from the moment it takes the keyboard until the switch is done (about a fifth of a second, 0.6 s at most) and then hands them to Spotlight in order; a key with ⌘, ⌃ or ⌥, or a click, lets them go at once. While another app holds Secure Input (a password field, or a login window that kept it after waking), macOS hides keys from RubberMAC, so they can't be held and a very quick first letter can still be lost. Esc goes back to searching everything (with letters typed, the first Esc only clears them), and Spotlight doesn't keep the filter for next time. Off until you turn it on; while off, nothing is watched.
  - Paste a path in Finder (⌘V or Ctrl-V) to go to that folder in the same window, without ⌘⇧G. A file's path goes to its folder and selects it. Plain paths, `~`, `file://` links, quoted paths and terminal-style `My\ Folder` all work. Pasting into a text box in Finder and pasting copied files work as before.
  - Delete in Finder moves the highlighted file to the Trash (⌘Z puts it back), with either delete key. Renaming, search, the address bar and the writing pad keep their delete key.
  - An address bar under the toolbar of the front Finder window shows the folder's path; paste or type a path there and press ↩ to go to it.
  - **When an app's Secure Input hides the keys:** while an app has Secure Input on (a password field or sign-in prompt, or a terminal's Secure Keyboard Entry), macOS hides the keys pressed with ⌘, Ctrl or Alt from RubberMAC, so window snapping, ⌘R, Ctrl-C/V, Alt-Tab and these other keys stop working. The first time you press ⌘, Ctrl or Alt then, RubberMAC puts an orange "!" on its menu-bar icon and a **Secure Input Is On** card at the top of the General and Keyboard pages that names the app holding it (read from the I/O Registry, as `ioreg` shows it) and says how to get the keys back, and hovering over the icon shows the same words; **Show** brings that app to the front, where its prompt may be hiding. Both go away within 3 seconds of Secure Input going off, and the log gets one line when it comes on and one when it goes off. Only while a keyboard feature is on; nothing runs while Secure Input is off.
- **Atkinson Hyperlegible:** the Braille Institute's typefaces for easy reading come with the app, Atkinson Hyperlegible Next and its monospaced sibling Atkinson Hyperlegible Mono. **Put Atkinson Hyperlegible in Every App's Font List**, on the Writing page, copies their 28 faces into `~/Library/Fonts`, as Font Book's Install does, and takes them out again; it never overwrites or removes a file that isn't its own copy. If the font is already installed some other way, the page says so instead. macOS's own menus and windows keep their font: that font is on the sealed system volume, and no app can change it.
  - **Use It In:** under the switch, one icon per app whose text can be set in it: the Writing Pad; TextEdit (new documents: Next for rich text, Mono for plain text); Mail (messages and the message list in Next, fixed-width text in Mono); Terminal (its default profile in Mono, open windows included); Chrome and Firefox (web pages that don't choose their own font, in every profile). A full-colour icon with a check is on, a faded one is off, and a click switches it. Sizes stay what each app had, and turning one off puts back exactly what it had before. TextEdit and Mail read their fonts as they start, so the change shows once they're opened again; Terminal changes at once, after macOS asks once whether RubberMAC may control it. Chrome and Firefox write their settings back from memory, so a click while one is open waits, and the change is made the moment it quits. Safari isn't there: it doesn't let web pages use fonts you install. Only installed apps are shown.
- **Writing pad:** ⌘⇧Space in Teams, Outlook or any app opens a pad (taken before Siri sees it, so holding it doesn't bring up Siri) to write the reply in. Apple's on-device model fixes it as you type, and ↩ pastes the fixed text where you were typing and leaves it on the clipboard. Each reply is saved with the text of the window it answered, read from the screen.
- **Mouse:**
  - Pointer speed up to 16 and wheel speed ×0.5–×8, each remembered per mouse model.
  - Hold the wheel and move to scroll.
  - **Wheel Scrolls the Classic Way:** turn the wheel towards you and the page goes down, as on Windows, while the trackpad keeps natural scrolling. With Natural scrolling off in System Settings the wheel already scrolls that way, and the Mouse page says so; turn Natural scrolling on later and the switch takes effect at once.
  - **Steady Wheel Scrolling, as on Windows:** every notch scrolls the same 3 lines however fast the wheel turns, in every app, Logitech mice through Logi Options+ included; the wheel speed still scales it. Trackpads, the Magic Mouse and mouse software's smooth scrolling are left alone.
  - **⌘ + wheel zooms in and out**, like Ctrl + wheel on Windows: away from you makes text bigger, towards you smaller, one step per notch, in Google Chrome, Claude, Safari, Terminal, TextEdit and any app with Zoom In and Zoom Out (or Bigger and Smaller) in its menus. Letting go of ⌘ afterwards doesn't open Start. Apps that can't zoom, design apps that zoom on ⌘ + scroll themselves, and remote desktops get the scroll as before. On by default; a switch on the Mouse page turns it off.
- **Window layouts:** remembers window positions for each monitor setup.
- **Feature requests:** **Send a Feature Request…**, on the General page, opens a small window to write an idea in (up to 2,000 characters), with your email if you'd like a reply. It goes to RubberMAC's developer with your RubberMAC and macOS versions and nothing else; no account is needed.
- **Updates:** checks [this repo's releases](../../releases) at launch and every six hours, then installs and relaunches.

## Install

Needs macOS 27 or later, on a Mac with Apple silicon.

1. Download `RubberMAC-<version>.dmg` from [Releases](../../releases), open it and drag RubberMAC to Applications.
2. Open RubberMAC. It lives in the menu bar, with no Dock icon.
3. It says why it needs Accessibility, then macOS asks: allow it under System Settings → Privacy & Security → Accessibility. The keyboard, window and Dock features need it.
4. Next it asks for Screen Recording, which is optional: it adds window pictures to Alt-Tab and the Dock menu, and the screen text the writing pad reads. Not Now leaves it for later, on the Permissions page of the settings panel. Apple Intelligence is what the writing pad's fixes need.

Everything runs on the Mac. Its only network traffic is checking GitHub for updates and downloading them, and a feature request when you send one, which carries only what you wrote and your RubberMAC and macOS versions.

## Build

```bash
./build.sh
```

`build.sh` compiles `src/*.swift` with `swiftc`, then installs `~/Applications/RubberMAC.app` (with the fonts in `fonts/`, a folder per family, in its Resources), signs it with the Developer ID and relaunches it. The Developer ID signature is what keeps the Accessibility grant across rebuilds.

## Release

1. Bump `CFBundleShortVersionString` and `CFBundleVersion` in `Info.plist`.
2. Add a `## <version>` section to `CHANGELOG.md`.
3. Run:

   ```bash
   ./release.sh
   ```

`release.sh` does the rest:

- runs `dmg.sh`, which builds into `build/`, signs with a secure timestamp, notarizes and staples;
- publishes `RubberMAC-<version>.zip` and the DMG as release `v<version>` on this repo (the one `origin` points to), using that version's changelog section as the notes;
- installs that same build into `~/Applications`.

One-time setup: run `xcrun notarytool store-credentials screentools`, be logged in to gh as the repo's owner, and name your Developer ID certificate in `.signing-identity` (ignored by git) or `SIGNING_IDENTITY`. `build.sh` needs that too.

To hand the app to someone without publishing an update, run `./dmg.sh` instead. It builds the version in `Info.plist` into `dist/RubberMAC-<version>.dmg` (the app and a link to Applications). It notarizes and staples the app, then the image, so both open on another Mac without a warning. It publishes nothing and leaves the installed app alone. It uses the same `screentools` notarytool profile.

The release is built and signed on this Mac, not in CI. Signing in CI would mean exporting the Developer ID key into GitHub secrets.

## Download counts

- `./downloads.sh` prints every release's downloads from GitHub's own count for each file. The DMG count is people downloading RubberMAC from the releases page. The zip count is mostly RubberMAC updating itself (see below), this Mac included.
- GitHub keeps only each file's running total. So the **Download Counts** workflow (`.github/workflows/downloads.yml`) records the counts once a day (05:17 UTC, or by hand from the Actions tab) on the `stats` branch:
  - `downloads.csv` holds every day's counts per file;
  - its `README.md` shows the totals per day and how many were added since the day before.
- The branch holds only those two files, and the workflow makes it on its first run. GitHub pauses scheduled workflows in a repo with no activity for 60 days.

## How updates are checked

- `UpdateFeed` reads GitHub's latest-release API for this repo. A release holds a zip, which the updater downloads, and a DMG for installing by hand.
- `UpdateInstaller` downloads the zip and unpacks it next to the installed app. It then refuses the download unless all of these hold:
  - its version matches the release;
  - its version is newer than the running one;
  - it passes the running copy's own code requirement, as macOS reads it from the signature: the same bundle ID, signed with the same developer's Developer ID.
- If every check passes, it swaps the new app in and relaunches.

## Feature request server

`server/requests` is what **Send a Feature Request…** posts to: a small Node server with no dependencies, hosted on AppCrane as `rubbermac-requests` (`FeatureRequest.server` holds its address). `POST /requests` takes `{text, email?, appVersion, macOS}`, checks it as the app does, and keeps it as one line of `requests.jsonl` in its data folder, with an id and the time and never the sender's address. A few an hour are taken from each address and 100 a day in all. `GET /` lists them for the app's owner behind AppCrane's sign-in, which only `/requests` is let past. With `NOTIFY_EMAIL` set, each request is also emailed there through AppCrane. `npm test` runs its tests.

The keyboard tap runs on a thread of its own, so typing never waits for the main thread (layout saves, Accessibility calls to a slow app). Its handlers decide there and hand any real work to the main queue; they read the frontmost app from `Frontmost`, not NSWorkspace.

`SpotlightFilterKeys` is the keyboard tap's last handler, so it holds keys as the others let them through (a Ctrl-C has become ⌘C by then and passes). It's on only with **Spotlight Searches Only Apps**; a hold starts when Spotlight's process announces a window taking the keyboard, 3-13 ms after the shortcut and before anyone can type, without asking Spotlight anything, and the held events are handed back with `postToPid` to Spotlight's process alone, inside the hold's lock, so a key arriving meanwhile waits and comes after them. Events posted that way don't pass through RubberMAC's taps again.

The ⌘ tap that opens Start counts only if nothing happened between ⌘'s press and release. `CommandTap` compares the system's event counters, and also `ScrollSeen`, the scrolls RubberMAC's mouse tap saw with a modifier held: the system's scroll counter misses the events Logi Options+ posts for its mice (measured with an MX Master 3), and a ⌘ + wheel zoom swallows its scrolls, so without it letting go of ⌘ would open Start.

Logs go to `~/Library/Logs/ScreenTools.log`. A key that waited more than 50 ms for the keyboard tap is logged as `keyboard tap: a key waited N ms` (never which key), at most once a second.

## Settings panel

- The menu-bar icon opens `SettingsPanel`, a `.transient` `NSPopover`; a click on the icon while it's open closes it. It activates the app so Esc and the search field work, and on closing with the keyboard goes back to the app that was in front.
- `AppDelegate.settingsPages()` (`SettingsPages.swift`) describes the pages as data (`SettingsPage`, `SettingsSection`, `SettingsRow`), afresh for each render, from the same conditions the menu used. Each row keeps its own `@objc` action; popups show copies of tagged `NSMenuItem`s, so the original arrives as the sender. A row can also hold its own view (`.custom`): the brightness and mouse sliders, the eye preview, and the eye colour circles (`EyeColorSwatches.swift`), whose colour names ride along as the row's unseen note so search finds them. `SettingsFacts` holds what they depend on besides `Settings`, so the self-test can build them under any condition.
- `SettingsRowView` draws one row and `SettingsDetailView` the page, in place when the page keeps its shape so a switch's animation isn't cut, and scrolling stays where it was. A re-render waits while a slider is being dragged.
- A row that takes over the screen, opens elsewhere or asks a question closes the panel first (`closesPanel`).
- `PermissionCheck` is the Permissions page's words for a reading, with no AppKit in it. Finder's side is read off the main thread (`FinderWindow.permission(ask: false)`), and the log has only `permissions: accessibility on, screen recording off, finder on`.
- The Eye Color Camera page reads `EyeCamera` through `EyeFacts`. Showing the page lists the cameras (`prepare()`) and checks once for camrelay's separate app, which would fight over the camera; while the page is open, each change of state re-renders it.
- The Eye Color Camera runs in RubberMAC: `EyeCapture` reads the chosen camera as 32BGRA frames on its own queue; for each, `EyePipeline` asks `EyeTracker` for the eyes (Vision's face landmarks, refined to the iris, smoothed over time as camrelay did), `IrisRecolor` recolours them in place (camrelay's formula, ported), and `VirtualCameraSink` queues the frame into OBS Virtual Camera's input stream (its CoreMediaIO sink, found by OBS's fixed device ID, as pyvirtualcam does). Colour, strength and camera live in `Settings`; `Settings.adoptCamrelaySettings()` copies camrelay's once.
- `EyePreview` shows the page's preview: an `AVCaptureSession` on OBS's camera extension (picked by model or maker, so a real camera is never opened) into an `AVCaptureVideoPreviewLayer`. `updateEyePreview()` runs it only while the panel is open on that page and the Eye Color Camera is sending a picture (`state == .on`), and stops it on any other page, on closing, and when the camera goes off.
- Ten features run on their own (`Feature`): `StartMenu`, `LevelOverlay`, `BackgroundDimmer`, `InputPerApp`, `DockScreen`, `WheelStyle`, `WindowReset`, `OpenOnScreen`, `ScreenResolution` and `SpotlightFilter`. `AppDelegate.features` starts each at launch and restarts its taps once Accessibility is on; each adds its own rows to a page from its own `…Settings.swift` (`startMenuRows`, `levelOverlaySections`, `backgroundDimSections`, `inputPerAppRows`, `dockScreenRows`), as the request form does on the General page (`featureRequestSections`).
- The panel's window belongs to RubberMAC, which the switcher, the Dock menu and saved layouts all leave out by process (and by `.regular` activation policy).

## Window switcher pictures

- The panel takes clicks without taking the focus (a non-activating panel, tiles accept the first click); a click on a tile picks that window, as letting go of Alt would. Without Screen Recording a line under the tiles says how to allow it.
- `SwitcherPanel` lays the windows out as a grid on up to 96% of the screen under the mouse, trying every column count for the largest tile (pictures up to 300 points wide) that fits them all; only below 60 points would it scroll. `SwitcherTileView` is one tile.
- `WindowThumbnails` takes a picture of each listed window, fitted to 300 × 188 points, with ScreenCaptureKit when the switcher opens, all at once, and hands each to `SwitcherPanel` as it arrives. The list opens with the previous pictures and the app icon, so it never waits for them. Pictures are held in memory until the next switch and never saved or logged; the log has only how many were taken, how many came from the window server and how long it took. ScreenCaptureKit takes no picture of a full-screen window whose Space isn't showing (error -3811), so for an off-screen window it lists but gives none for, the picture comes from the window server instead (`SLSHWCaptureWindowList`, private, as AltTab uses it), shrunk to the same size. A window it leaves off its list gets no picture, and neither does one with `NSWindow.sharingType` `.none`, which is checked before that call.
- `WindowMapView` draws the screens in their arrangement, shrunk to fit, with the window at its place (its frame comes from Accessibility with the title, in the same round trip). It's the small map in each tile's caption, and fills the tile, with the app icon in the window's place, until there's a picture. Minimized windows are faded.
- `FocusHistory` is saved in the defaults (`focusHistory`) with the boot time, since window IDs start over at each boot.

## Windows on other Spaces

- Accessibility lists only an app's windows on the current Space. `SpaceWindows` asks the window server (private SkyLight calls, loaded with `dlsym` so a missing one falls back to the current Space only) for every window on each user Space, and which are ordered in; a window ordered out is a closed one the app keeps, and isn't listed.
- `SpaceElements` finds the Accessibility element of a window on another Space by its element number (`_AXUIElementCreateWithRemoteToken`), in the background, and caches it per app: when focus tracking starts, after a Space change, and after a snapshot finds a window it doesn't have.
- Picking such a window brings its app forward on that window (`_SLPSSetFrontProcessWithOptions`), which switches Space, then raises it.

## Switching desktops

- macOS 27 ignores a posted ⌃←/→, Mission Control's own Move Left/Right a Space, so ⌃⌘←/→ can't press it the way ⌘R presses Spotlight's shortcut. `DesktopSwipe` hands the Dock a made-up trackpad swipe instead, translated from [InstantSpaceSwitcher](https://github.com/jurplel/InstantSpaceSwitcher) (MIT): for each of began, changed and ended, a DockControl event carrying the raw IOHID gesture payload macOS 27 wants, and a Gesture event beside it, posted at session level. Near-zero progress and a fling on ended switch at once. The sign flips with natural scrolling. The CGEvent field numbers are undocumented and could change with macOS.
- A swipe past the last desktop wedges the Dock on macOS 27 (a black screen until it gives up), so `Desktops` reads the row of Spaces of the screen with the menu bar first and makes no swipe at either end. Right after a swipe the window server can still report the Space it left, so for 1 s the Space it went to counts as the current one.

## Full-screen Dock

- `FullScreenDock` watches pointer moves on a tap of its own thread. When the pointer reaches the Dock's edge (from `com.apple.dock`'s `orientation`) and the front window is full screen (`AXFullScreen`), it posts a few moves that keep going past the edge, as a push does, until the Dock is up or the pointer leaves.

## Dock window menu

- `DockClicks` is a handler on the pointer tap (session level, its own thread). On a left press it asks the Dock alone, through Accessibility, what's under the pointer (`DockItems`: about 0.1 ms, and only the Dock's own icons answer, so other clicks pay nearly nothing); a hidden Dock's icons sit below the screen's edge, where no click lands. For a running app's icon (`AXApplicationDockItem`, `AXIsApplicationRunning`) that a system-wide hit test also gives to the Dock (nothing over it there), it holds back the press and the release.
- `DockWindowPicker` lists that app's windows, as Alt-Tab does, while the button is down. With two or more it opens `DockWindowMenu` beside the icon; with fewer it hands the click on to the Dock (a press and a release where it was made, marked so the tap lets them through). Moving more than 4 points while pressed hands on the press and then the move, so the Dock sees a drag.
- `DockWindowMenu` is a non-activating panel; each row (`DockWindowRow`) shows the window's picture from `WindowThumbnails` (or its place on the screens with the app icon until there is one) and its title. Picking goes through `WindowFocus`, shared with Alt-Tab. Esc is taken by `DockMenuKeys` on the keyboard tap only while the menu is open.
- Under each title, `ProcessUsage` gives the app's CPU and memory (`proc_pid_rusage`: user + system time, including children already waited for, and `ri_phys_footprint`), summed over the process and everything it started (`proc_listchildpids`, recursively). The times are Mach ticks, converted with `mach_timebase_info`. Samples are taken off the main thread, first when the menu opens and then every second, since walking a tree of a hundred processes takes milliseconds. When the windows come from more than one process, each row also names its copy from that process's `--user-data-dir` (read with `KERN_PROCARGS2`). Nothing of it is logged.

## Finder paste

- `FinderPathPaste` is a keyboard-tap handler after `ClipboardKeys`, so Ctrl-V reaches it as ⌘V. It takes the ⌘V only when Finder is frontmost, the clipboard holds text (not copied files) naming something that exists, and Finder's focused element isn't a text box.
- `FinderWindow` moves the front Finder window with Apple Events (`set target of Finder window 1`, then `select` for a file), off the main thread. Under the hardened runtime this needs the `com.apple.security.automation.apple-events` entitlement (`ScreenTools.entitlements`, passed to `codesign` by `build.sh` and `release.sh`) and the user's one-time OK to control Finder, asked on the first paste.
- With the desktop in front instead of a folder window, or without that OK, the path opens in a new Finder window. Once the OK is refused, the Keyboard page offers the Automation settings.
- The log records which of these happened, never the path.
- `FinderAddressBar` puts a non-activating panel under the front Finder window's toolbar (or tab bar), kept just above that window and hidden when Finder isn't in front. It reads the folder with `FinderWindow.currentFolder()` and follows the window with an AXObserver. ↩ goes by the same rules as a pasted path.

## Writing pad

- `WritingAssistant` opens the pad over the focused window of the frontmost app and remembers that window.
- `ScreenText` captures that one window with ScreenCaptureKit and reads its text with Vision. This needs the Screen Recording permission; without it the pad still fixes text, just without the screen as context.
- `TextFixer` sends the draft, with the end of the screen text as context, to Apple's on-device model (FoundationModels). Nothing leaves the Mac. Fixing needs Apple Intelligence turned on.
- `WritingPadKeys` takes ⌘⇧Space in the keyboard tap, ahead of Siri's own watcher, which ignores ⇧ and would otherwise read a held ⌘⇧Space as its "hold ⌘Space". The Carbon hotkey stays as the fallback when the tap isn't running.
- `WritingPad` is a non-activating panel, so the app being replied in stays frontmost and keeps its cursor. `Paster` pastes with ⌘V into that app, and only that app. The sent text stays on the clipboard.
- `ReplyStore` appends every sent reply to `~/Library/Application Support/Screen Tools/Replies/<year>-<month>.jsonl`, one JSON object per line: `date`, `app`, `bundleID`, `screen` (the window's text), `typed`, `sent`. The folder is readable only by you.
- The log records that a reply was sent and its length, never its text or the screen's.
